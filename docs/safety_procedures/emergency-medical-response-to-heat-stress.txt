Heat stress can develop before a person collapses. Watch for headache, unusual irritability, weakness, cramps, heavy sweating, confusion, or a worker who stops answering normally. In hot areas, use the planned work-rest schedule, hydration point, shade, and buddy checks. Do not wait for the person to request relief; new workers often feel pressure to continue.

If a worker shows confusion, collapse, seizure, or very hot skin, treat it as a medical emergency. Call the site emergency number, move others away, and cool the person using the approved first-aid method while waiting for medical staff. Do not give fluids to an unconscious or confused person. Do not send the person home alone.

For milder symptoms, stop the task, move the worker to a cool area, loosen clothing, and provide water or electrolyte drink only if the person is alert and able to swallow. Medical evaluation is still required under the site rule. Record the task, area temperature if available, PPE, duration, breaks, and any contributing illness or medication information voluntarily provided to medical staff.

Veterans sometimes call dizziness “just furnace heat.” That attitude causes delays. A person who is normally strong can deteriorate quickly after dehydration, poor sleep, or a new heat load. Production can wait for a proper check.
